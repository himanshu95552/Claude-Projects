| Part | Slides | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Who we are | 1 to 4 | 4 min | Earn the right to their attention |
| 2. The world you already know | 6 to 9 | 6 min | Get them nodding at their own operation |
| 3. The demonstration | 10 to 20 | 24 min | Show one order go from referral to report to payment |
| 4. Close | 21 to 25 | 7 min | Show what it is worth, then make the next step easy |
| Questions | | 4 min | |

Slide 5 was cut in v1.2. Slide 21 (the unit-economics closer) was added in v1.3 and rebased on a normalized representative center in v1.4. Slide 17 (the visit and the report) was added in v2.1, and the demo budget is now A 4 min · B 6 · C 5 · D 3 · E 3 · F 3.

**The discovery question that steers the demo.** On slide 7, ask which of the three leaks costs them the most. Spend the most demo time on their answer.

**One demo patient, one order, the whole way through.** Use a realistic fictional patient (for example, "Maria Lopez, 52, knee MRI, referred by Dr. Patel's office"). Do not reuse cartoon characters like the Mickey Mouse and Donald Duck names in the 2025 screenshots; they undercut the seriousness of the demo and are someone else's characters.

**The buyer is the patient.** Maria's texts go to a phone in the room, and Gravity's voice agent calls that phone as the scheduler, on speaker. Tell the buyer: "Don't baby it. Interrupt it. Change your mind." In the recorded pitches, this unscripted moment produced the strongest reactions of any demo segment; graceful handling under pressure is itself the proof.

## Part 2. The world you already know

### Slide 6. A day at your center

**On screen**
- Headline: **You already know this picture.**
- Build 1, three questions: How many referrals went elsewhere last month? · How many orders never became exams? · How many exams were never fully paid?
- Build 2, scenes: a full fax tray · phones on hold · a payer portal open in every tab · a scheduler re-keying an order · a denial from six weeks ago
- The stack underneath: CRM or spreadsheet · RIS · RCM · phone system · fax server · texting vendor · interfaces · servers

**Talk track**
> "Let me ask you three questions, and it's fine if you can't answer them. Most centers can't. How many referrals went to another center last month? How many orders arrived and never became exams? How many exams did you complete and never get fully paid for? Not because your team is bad. Because the answers live in three different systems, and nobody has ever added them up.
>
> Here's the picture I see at most centers. The fax tray is full. The phones are backed up. Someone has four payer portals open. A scheduler is typing an order from a fax into the RIS by hand. And a denial just arrived for an exam you did six weeks ago. Kind of describes your current life?
>
> Quick question: how many systems does one order touch before you get paid?"

**Note:** let them answer both questions. The system count is usually higher than they expect once the phone, fax and texting tools are counted. Never frame the staff as the problem: they are the people holding it together.

### Slide 7. Every order can be lost three times

**On screen**
- Headline: **Every order can be lost three times.**

>
> Which of those three costs you the most today?"

**Note:** their answer decides where the demo spends its time.

### Slide 8. Why nobody has fixed it

**On screen**
- Headline: **Each leak lives in a different system.**
- The CRM sees referrals. The RIS sees schedules. The RCM sees claims.
- **Nobody sees the whole order.**
- So people close the gaps by hand, and growth means hiring.
- Build 2, the model-break: **Airlines fill about 85% of their seats. Imaging centers fill about 65% of their slots. And patients still wait weeks.**
- Footnote: Airlines: US Bureau of Transportation Statistics, 2019 load factor. Imaging: measured MRI slot utilization at a multi-site outpatient group, 2020 (Tower Radiology, RSNA 2021).

**Talk track**
> "The reason these leaks persist isn't that anyone's doing a bad job. It's that each one lives in a different system, owned by a different team. Your referral data doesn't know about your denials. Your schedule doesn't know which referrer's orders keep getting rejected. So your people carry information between systems all day. And when volume goes up, the only answer has been to hire.
>
> Count the systems. A CRM, if you have one. A RIS. A billing system. None of them talk. Underneath them, a phone system, a fax server, a texting vendor, an interface engine. Every system you add is another place for work to leak, another vendor to manage, and another door to secure.
>
> And here's the number that should bother you. Airlines fill about 85 percent of their seats. Imaging centers fill about 65 percent of their slots. And patients still wait weeks for an MRI. If you were short of capacity you'd be at 90. You're not short of scanners. You're short of orders that make it all the way through. You don't have a capacity problem. You have a leakage problem."

**Status:** `LOCKED` 23 Sep 2026. Hotels dropped (no source). "Every system you add is another place for work to leak" approved (positioning decision 13).

### Segment A. Get the order: CRM and referral acquisition

#### Slide 10. No referral leaks

**On screen**
- Headline: **No referral leaks: get the order.**
- Today: referrers fax or send elsewhere; nobody notices a referrer going quiet until the volume is gone; their office calls for a status and waits on hold; the report comes back by fax days later.
- With Gravity: every referral is captured, whether it arrives through the portal or by fax. The office's call is always answered. They see the order's progress in real time. The report and the images are back the moment the read is signed. And you know who refers, who stopped, and why.
- Line: **We don't just run your orders. We go get them.**
- Second line: **Every referral captured. Every report returned.**

**Talk track**
> "Most operations software starts when the order arrives. We start before that, because for a lot of centers, the real problem is that the orders aren't coming in. Referring offices send their orders where it's easy, where they can see what's happening, and where the report comes back fast. Gravity's agents act as your liaison to every referring office: the order comes in however they send it, their call is always answered, they see the progress, and the report and images land back the moment they're signed. Your liaisons spend their time in the office, not on the fax machine. And you know who refers, who stopped, and why."

#### Slide 11. Demo: an order arrives

**Demo flow**
1. Open the referrer view in the CRM. Show a practice whose volume has dropped over the last month, flagged automatically. "This practice sent you forty orders a month for a year, and this month it sent twelve. Gravity flagged it on day nine. Most centers find out a quarter later, in the volume report, after the orders have a new home."
2. Show outreach going to that office by fax, phone or text from inside Gravity.
3. Switch to the referring physician portal. Dr. Patel's office submits Maria's knee MRI order digitally.
4. Show the order landing in Gravity instantly, with no fax and no re-keying.
5. Show what Dr. Patel's office sees from here on: the order's status in the portal (received, scheduled, authorized, completed, report ready), updated as the demo goes. "Nobody in that office will call you for a status. They can see it. And if they do call or text, Gravity answers."

**The moment:** a referrer going quiet is noticed in days, not quarters; the order arrives already digital; and the office can see everything that happens to it without a phone call.

### Slide 21. Your exam, itemized

**New in v1.3; rebased in v1.4; per exam only in v2.0.** The unit-economics closer: one exam as a P&L, today and with Gravity. The unit is the exam, never the center, because most buyers run more than one site and the per-exam economics hold for one center or twenty. No real center's figures are shown anywhere. Full model, evidence, objections and data intake in [[AN27 — Unit Economics Closer]] (v1.1).

**On screen**
- Headline: **Your exam, itemized.**
- Left column, *Today:* Revenue $154 · Get the order $11 · Get the patient in $16 · The scan $44 · The read $21 · Get paid $13 · The building $39 · **Net $11**
- Right column, *With Gravity:* Revenue $156 · Get the order $7 · Get the patient in $8 · The scan $42 · The read $21 · Get paid $7 · The building $36 · Gravity $3.45 · **Net $31**
- Bridge: **$11 an exam to $31. A 7% margin to 20%. Same scanners. Same referrers.**
- Strip: Between the referral and the payment: **$39** an exam today, **$26** with Gravity.
- Footnote: One blended outpatient exam across eight modalities, modeled on the cost structure of independent outpatient imaging; not any one center's figures. Expected case; conservative case $21 an exam (14% margin).

**Visual:** an itemized statement, two columns, typographic. No pipes, funnels or leaking anything; the leak is a line item. If a chart is wanted, one pair of stacked horizontal bars against a revenue line. Stage values round to the dollar; design carries the net from the model rather than summing rounded rows.

**Talk track**
> "Let me put the whole argument on one exam. Not a center, one exam, because that's the unit that's the same whether you run one site or twenty. A blended outpatient exam collects about $154. Here is where it goes. Forty-four dollars to do the scan: the technologist, the machine, the service contract. Twenty-one to read it. Thirty-nine for the building and running the business. And another thirty-nine dollars to get the order, get the patient in the door, and get paid: the people, the phones, the RIS. Twenty-nine of that thirty-nine is people who never touch a patient. You pay twice as much to move the paper as to read the scan.
>
> That leaves eleven dollars. Seven percent. Normal for an independent, and fragile: eighty percent of that cost is fixed, paid whether the table is full or not. Lose eight percent of your volume and that eleven dollars becomes two.
>
> The same exam with Gravity. The paper drops from thirty-nine dollars to about twenty-six, and that includes what you pay us. The empty slots fill, so the fixed cost spreads over more exams, and every extra exam is worth a hundred and twenty-seven dollars because the scanner is already paid for. Eleven dollars becomes thirty-one. Seven percent becomes twenty. Get half of that on every line and it is still twenty-one dollars an exam.
>
> Multiply by your exam count. I don't know your numbers. You do. That is the next step."

**Landing pad:** CFO and CEO first; the administrator hears the twenty-nine dollars of people; the CIO hears the RIS line sitting inside "get the patient in."
