# Why denials show up weeks after the scan, and what was visible at booking

*For billing and revenue-cycle leads*

A denial feels like a billing problem because that's where it lands. Someone in billing opens a queue, sees a claim that was rejected, and starts working backward. The visit was weeks ago. The patient has gone home. The person who booked the exam may not remember it.

But most denials start much earlier than the claim. They start when an order was entered, a patient was booked, or an authorization was assumed to be in place. The information that would have prevented the denial was usually available at that moment. It just wasn't acted on.

## A real example

MRI Associates, a multi-site imaging group, found that Medicare was entered as the primary insurance on a large share of appointments where it should have been secondary. By their account, close to a third of appointments could show the error.

Two things caused it. Patients sometimes misunderstood their own coverage and reported Medicare as primary. And booking staff, working under pressure, typed in what the patient told them. Nothing flagged the mismatch.

The wrong payer then flowed into everything after it: the estimate, the prior authorization, the claim. Payments were delayed or denied. Some appointments were rescheduled while billing sorted it out.

The odd part is that the eligibility check had often already shown Medicare as secondary. That result just never reached the RIS, so nobody acted on it. The information existed. It was in the wrong place.

## Five checks that belong at booking

If a denial is going to happen, one of these is usually where it starts. Check them when the patient books, not when the claim comes back.

1. **Who is primary.** Confirm the order of coverage against an eligibility check, not only against what the patient says.
2. **Whether the exam needs an authorization, and whether you have it.** Match the exam and the payer to your rules, and make sure the approval covers the date of service.
3. **Whether the order supports the exam.** Check the order and the notes against the payer's medical necessity criteria before you submit, not after a rejection.
4. **Whether the referring provider is eligible.** For Medicare, a referring provider who isn't enrolled can cause a denial, and an ABN may be needed.
5. **What the patient will owe.** Work out the estimate once coverage is known and tell the patient before the visit.

None of these is complicated. What makes them hard is that they live in different places, and a busy desk skips whichever one isn't obviously urgent.

## How to find your own starting point

Pull your last month of denials and sort them by the reason code. For each of the top few reasons, ask one question: could we have known this when the patient booked? In most centers the answer is yes more often than people expect, and the answer tells you which of the five checks to tighten first.

Then follow one denied claim all the way back to the referral. Write down where the missing fact first appeared. That single trace usually shows you more than a month of reports.
