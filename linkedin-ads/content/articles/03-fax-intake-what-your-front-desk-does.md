# Fax intake: what your front desk actually does with every order

*For operations and practice managers*

Ask a front desk lead how orders arrive and you'll often hear "fax." Ask what happens next and the answer takes longer. It's worth writing the whole thing down, because the steps are the cost.

## The path of one faxed order

Someone opens the fax. If it contains more than one patient, they split the pages and put them in order. They read it to find the patient and look the patient up, which means checking the name, the date of birth and sometimes the phone number. They work out which exam is being ordered and what the diagnosis and codes are. They key all of that into the system.

Then they check what's missing. Maybe the referring provider's details are incomplete, or there are no clinical notes, or the order needs an authorization. When something is missing, they build a cover sheet listing the gaps, fax it back, and track whether the office answers. If the office doesn't, they call.

Only after all that does the order become something you can book.

## Why it adds up

One order, done carefully, takes minutes. The work isn't hard. It's repetitive and it never stops. One operations director described their volume as six to seven thousand faxes a month, four pages each. At that scale, the fax queue isn't a side task. It is most of someone's day, and the work waiting behind it is the day's real work.

It also hides risk. A typo in a date of birth, a wrong exam, a missed page: each one creates a problem that someone else finds later, often at the scanner or in billing.

## What to measure

You can learn a lot from three numbers, and none of them needs software:

- How many orders are waiting to be entered right now, and how long has the oldest one waited?
- How many orders each week need a call or a fax back for missing information?
- How many of those end up rescheduled or delayed?

Track them for two weeks. Together they tell you how much of your front desk's time goes to moving information from one place to another.

## What changes when software reads the fax

Software can do the reading, matching and filling in, and hand a person a draft to check.

In Gravity, faxes land in a document inbox on the center's own number. The system classifies each page, reads the patient, insurance, provider and exam details, and files each page to the right record. Missing items are flagged, requested from the referring office, and tracked.

Centers choose how much to trust it. Some have a person review every order. Others let routine orders finish on their own and look only at exceptions.

The work doesn't disappear. It changes shape. People stop copying and start deciding.
