Alpha Nodus · LinkedIn B2B campaign · prepared 21 Oct 2026

Talk like the front desk. Land on one page.

A three-level LinkedIn plan for owners, managers, billing leads and radiologists at independent imaging centers. The ads are written in the language of the people who run these centers. Every click goes to one landing page where they book a free 30-minute Order Trace.

The plan in one page

Describe a moment the reader has lived, show we understand the cost, then ask for something small: a 30-minute call where we trace one of their real orders.

Goal

Book qualified calls

Primary conversion: a Calendly booking. Secondary: content leads that retargeting can warm up.

Audience

Four buyers, one center

Owner, operations manager, revenue-cycle lead, radiologist-owner. Each gets the leak that hurts them most.

Offer

The Order Trace

Cold: a 15-question checklist. Warm: a free 30-minute working session that maps the order from referral to payment.

Creative principles

  • One moment per ad. The first line (visible before "see more") must be a situation the reader recognizes.
  • Every ad names its reader in the first line and carries a role tag on the image. The "Make it theirs" tab has the full rules.
  • Write like the operator talks: faxes, front desk, call-backs, denials, auth, A/R. No product-module names, no slogans from the site, no "AI-powered".
  • The founder's real story earns attention, but the post must end up in his own words. I drafted it for him to rewrite.
  • Photos show places and hands (fax tray, front desk, scanner room), not stock smiles. Never show a patient or readable patient data.
  • Healthcare compliance: keep the LinkedIn Insight Tag off any consumer or patient-facing page. LinkedIn's own guidance says to exclude pages with health-related consumer services. This is not legal advice. LinkedIn help.

Simple ads: ask the question, answer with the fix

Each ad does three things: asks one plain question the right person answers "yes" to, answers it with the one thing Gravity does about it, and sends them to the page that explains that fix. Sixteen ads, one pain each.

The question
  • Twelve words or fewer.
  • One pain, in the words they use.
  • Starts with "Still…", "Do you…", "Which…" or names the symptom.
  • Names no product and no jargon.
The answer
  • One sentence: what Gravity does, with a verb.
  • Matches the question exactly: fax question, fax answer.
  • Never "solves everything". Staff review the exceptions.
  • Proof only from approved customer stories.
The picture
  • One scene from their day that shows the pain.
  • Drawn here as an illustration. Each ad has a photo prompt that turns the same scene into a realistic photo.
  • No faces, no readable text, a freestanding center, never a hospital.

Make every ad say "this is for you"

The test is two seconds. A center owner or billing lead should know the ad is about them before they read a full sentence, and everyone else should feel free to scroll. These are the eight rules every ad and variant here follows.

1. Name the reader in the first line

LinkedIn shows roughly the first 150 characters before "see more". That line is the whole audience filter.

2. One role tag on every image

Same position (top-left), same style, same wording as the first line. People who only glance at the picture still get the message.

FOR CENTER OWNERSFOR CENTER OPERATIONSFOR BILLING & RCM LEADSFOR RADIOLOGIST-OWNERSFOR MRI CENTERSFOR FLORIDA CENTERS

Rules: all caps, "FOR" plus at most four words, one tag per ad, no tag on ads that speak to everyone except as "FOR IMAGING CENTERS".

3. Show their objects, not stock healthcare

Do show
  • A fax tray with paper orders, a requisition clipboard, a sticky-note-covered monitor.
  • A small front desk with a tech in scrubs (no faces).
  • A freestanding center: plaza storefront, one or two scanners, a modest waiting room.
  • A schedule grid, an authorization queue, a denial worklist (blurred or demo data).
  • A real screenshot of your product with demo data (ad C6).
Avoid
  • Hospital lobbies, campus signs, giant atriums: they say "not you".
  • Smiling doctors with tablets, glowing brain graphics, robots.
  • Any readable patient name, date of birth or scan.
  • Logos or brand names that are not yours.
  • Faces of people who are not real, consenting employees or customers.

5. Match their size and modality

The hooks below are hypotheses. Check them with two or three of your sales or customer-success people before launch.

By monthly exams (same bands as your pricing)

  • Up to 1,000: one or two people run the whole front end.
  • 1,001–5,000: growth has outrun the process.
  • 5,001–20,000: more than one site or shift; orders fall between handoffs.
  • 20,000+: talk to sales directly; use the account list and personal outreach, not broad ads.

By modality

  • MRI: authorizations and slots that are hard to refill.
  • CT: same-day add-ons and shifting schedules.
  • X-ray / ultrasound / multi-modality: walk-in traffic and high order volume at the desk.

See the Variants tab for the ready-made ads.

6. Make it local

Split ad sets by state and name the place in the first line: "Florida imaging center managers:". Alpha Nodus has offices in Sunrise, Florida and Austin, Texas, so those two can add "our team is nearby". Other states get the callout without the proximity line. If a state has too few matched accounts to reach LinkedIn's 300 minimum, group neighbors (for example "Southeast").

7. Prove it with someone like them

Peer-proof template (B4 and B5 already follow it with real stories):

"[Center type, scanner count, region]. Before: [one concrete problem, in their words]. After: [one concrete result with a number and a time frame]." — [Role], [approved name or "a customer"]

Label by size and region even if they do not want a name. Written approval for every name, quote and logo, including association logos like AHRA, RBMA and RSNA.

8. Personalize inside the format

  • Conversation ads: first-name merge in the greeting. Check in Campaign Manager whether a company-name merge is offered to you.
  • One ad per role: each of the four buyers has a headline and image that only fits them. Do not combine roles in one ad.
  • Lead form: pre-filled name, title and company make the form feel made for them. Add the exam-volume question.
  • Landing page: headline swaps by role using the ?role= tag, so the page echoes the ad.

Check that it works

Before launch: the 5-person test

  1. Show each ad for 3 seconds with the logo covered to five people who work in imaging centers.
  2. Ask: "Who is this for? What's it about?"
  3. At least four of five should name the right role and problem. If not, rewrite the first line and the tag.

After launch: the signals

  • CTR by job-title segment: the intended role should win.
  • Lead form titles: if the wrong titles fill it, the callout is too loose.
  • Comments: do people reply with their own story? That is the strongest sign of recognition.
  • Company-size mix of leads versus the exam-volume question.

Setup reference: LinkedIn Matched Audiences best practices.

Setup, in order

Do steps 1–6 before spending a dollar. Most failed B2B campaigns skip the tracking.

  1. Confirm the foundations

    Company Page complete, Campaign Manager ad account with billing, and an admin with Content Admin rights so thought-leader posts can be boosted.

  2. Install the LinkedIn Insight Tag on B2B pages only

    Homepage, Gravity solution pages, pricing, About, demo. Keep it off any consumer or patient-facing page. Verify in Campaign Manager that the tag is firing.

  3. Create the booking conversion

    Embed the booking calendar on the landing page and send people to a thank-you page after booking (check that your Calendly plan supports a custom redirect). Track that page as the conversion. Add a second conversion for form submits. Set a 30-day click and 7-day view window.

  4. Build the audiences

    Upload the account list and customer exclusion list. Create the retargeting pools from the table in the Targeting tab. New pools take time to reach the 300-member minimum, so they start small.

  5. Create three campaign groups

    "L1 Awareness", "L2 Consideration", "L3 Decision". Budget, reporting, and pausing happen at this level.

  6. Set the campaign objectives

    L1: Brand awareness for single image and Video views for the video. L2: Lead generation. L3: Website conversions. Start with maximum delivery bidding; move to manual bidding only if costs run away after two weeks.

  7. Upload creatives with naming

    Name format: L2_Doc_Checklist_RCM_v1. Run three or four ads per campaign. UTM: utm_source=linkedin&utm_medium=paid&utm_campaign=<level>&utm_content=<ad id>.

  8. Connect lead delivery

    Send Lead Gen Form submissions to your CRM or email within minutes. Speed matters: follow up the same day with the Calendly link.

  9. Add variants only after the base ads have data

    Launch A1 and C1 first. Add the modality, region and size variants from the Variants tab as separate ad sets once their audiences clear 300 members.

  10. Launch order

    Week 1: L1 and L2 on the account list. Week 2: switch on L3 retargeting once pools pass 300. Week 3: first creative pruning. Week 4+: add the two customer-story ads once approved.

  11. Weekly rhythm

    Review CTR and cost per lead weekly; review booked calls and show rate every two weeks; refresh creative every 3–4 weeks (audiences are small, so fatigue shows up fast). Cap frequency by watching it per audience.

Budget and what to measure

Recommended start: $10,000 a month for 90 days, then reallocate. Below about $6,000 the retargeting pools are too thin to learn from.

Reading the economics: pricing starts at $1,000 a month, so a customer is worth at least $12,000 a year. If a booked call costs around $343 (the vendor-reported median for demo-type leads) and one in four calls becomes a customer, the cost per customer is roughly $1,400 against $12,000+ in first-year revenue. Those two rates are my assumptions; replace them with your close rate once you have data.
